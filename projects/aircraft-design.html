01 — PROJECT OVERVIEW

Three complementary studies in preliminary aircraft design

This case study brings together three team assignments completed for the Aircraft Design course at Universidad Alfonso X el Sabio. They address different design problems and, importantly, two different aircraft concepts: the first study develops a new single-aisle aircraft sized around a 170-passenger, 4,000 km mission; the second estimates the cruise drag polar for a reference transport-aircraft geometry; and the third redesigns the propulsion system and wing of an Airbus A350. They should therefore be understood as a sequence of design methods and coursework milestones, not as one continuous specification for the same aircraft.

Together, the reports cover requirements interpretation, benchmarking against existing aircraft, cabin and fuselage packaging, aerodynamic drag estimation, constraint analysis, propulsion selection and wing planform definition. 02 — STUDY 01

Fuselage dimensioning and cabin arrangement

Design brief. The first assignment established the preliminary dimensions of a single-aisle transport aircraft intended to carry approximately 170 passengers over a design range of 4,000 km. Rather than starting with arbitrary dimensions, the team benchmarked four comparable aircraft: the Boeing 737-900, Airbus A321, COMAC C919 and McDonnell Douglas MD-83. Their published airport-planning and cabin data provided reference values for fuselage length and width, seat dimensions, aisle width, seat pitch and cargo volume.

Cabin architecture. The selected layout contains 16 Business Class seats and 156 Economy Class seats. Business uses a 2–2 arrangement, while Economy uses a 3–3 arrangement with one aisle. The report uses average seat widths, armrest widths, aisle widths and seat pitches from the reference aircraft to estimate the cabin cross-section and cabin length. The resulting seat pitches are 0.94 m in Business and 0.80 m in Economy; corresponding seat widths are 0.55 m and 0.47 m.

Fuselage dimensions. The calculated cabin width is 3.66 m and the external fuselage width is approximately 3.76 m. The cabin length is 30.4 m, with an estimated total fuselage length of 42.517 m. The resulting fineness ratio, defined as fuselage length divided by fuselage width, is 10.564. The report also estimates a passenger comfort volume of 1.628 m³ per passenger and a cargo-hold volume of 35.387 m³, using a Nordisk AKH (LD3-45) unit load device as a packaging reference.

Safety and packaging considerations. The preliminary layout considers emergency-exit distribution and spacing with reference to CS-25 requirements. The report discusses the need to account for flight-deck volume, passenger-service areas, lavatories, galleys, overhead and underfloor packaging, structural lining thickness and cargo accommodation. These are conceptual sizing results; a full compliance demonstration would require a more detailed cabin safety and evacuation assessment.

CAD development. The team modelled the aircraft and cabin arrangements in Autodesk Fusion 360. The extracted views show cabin cross-sections, seating arrangements, cargo packaging and the overall aircraft geometry. They provide visual evidence of how the analytical dimensions were translated into a preliminary three-dimensional configuration.

03 — STUDY 02

Estimation of the aircraft cruise drag polar

Objective and flight condition. The second assignment estimates the drag polar and maximum aerodynamic efficiency for a transport-aircraft configuration in cruise. The specified condition is 36,000 ft (10,972.93 m) and 985 km/h (273.61 m/s), using a NACA 63-209 wing section. The reference geometry includes a 36.28 m span, 4.13 m mean geometric chord, 4.76 m mean aerodynamic chord, 26° leading-edge sweep and 0.33 taper ratio. The fuselage reference is 43.58 m long and 4.02 m wide.

Atmosphere and similarity parameters. The report converts the input data to SI units and applies the International Standard Atmosphere to obtain 216.83 K, 12,436.41 Pa and a density of 0.19985 kg/m³. These values give a cruise Mach number of approximately 0.93. Reynolds number is evaluated for both the flight condition and a cut-off/roughness criterion; the lower, more conservative value is retained for the aerodynamic calculations.

Parasite drag. The wing and fuselage are first treated separately. The wing estimate uses a flat-plate analogy and includes skin-friction, form-factor and wave-drag contributions. The NACA 63-209 section is used to estimate maximum thickness ratio, while airfoil data are used to derive the profile drag relationship. The fuselage estimate follows a similar approach, with its wetted area, fineness ratio, base area and base drag treated explicitly. The report then combines the component contributions to obtain the zero-lift parasite-drag term.

Induced drag and balanced polar. The induced-drag model accounts for finite-wing tip vortices, viscous/separation effects and the influence of the horizontal tailplane. The resulting parabolic polar is reported as CD = 0.017789 + 0.052013 CL². The constant term represents the estimated zero-lift drag contribution; the coefficient multiplying CL² represents the lift-dependent induced-drag contribution in the adopted model. From these coefficients, the report calculates a maximum lift-to-drag ratio of approximately 16.44.

This polar is a compact engineering model: for a specified lift coefficient, it estimates the corresponding drag coefficient under the assumptions and reference condition of the report. It is useful for preliminary performance calculations, but should not be interpreted as a measured flight-test polar.

04 — STUDY 03

Powerplant selection and Airbus A350 wing redesign

Design objective. The third assignment considers an Airbus A350 at 35,000 ft and 985 km/h (Mach 0.923). The task is to select an alternative propulsion system and resize the wing so that the aircraft satisfies the analysed operating requirements. The study constructs a constraint diagram with thrust-to-weight ratio on one axis and wing loading on the other. Each flight phase imposes a different boundary on the feasible design region.

Performance constraints. The take-off analysis relates required thrust-to-weight ratio to wing loading, take-off field length and maximum lift coefficient. The landing analysis imposes a wing-loading limit based on landing field length and landing maximum lift coefficient. The second-segment climb case checks the climb gradient with the critical engine inoperative, a particularly important multi-engine transport-aircraft requirement. The cruise analysis estimates the thrust required to sustain the specified cruise condition. Overlaying these constraints identifies combinations that satisfy all the studied conditions.

Design point and engine. The report compares candidate engines and selects the General Electric GE90-115B, reporting 513,947 N thrust per engine and an engine mass of 8,761 kg. The selected thrust-to-weight ratio is 0.3742 and the selected wing loading is 8,000 Pa. The report then obtains a wing reference area of 343.35 m², an aspect ratio of 8.426 and a span of 53.788 m. These are the values reported by the coursework; they are not independent certification or performance-validation results.

Wing geometry. The wing is developed as a double-taper planform with a 33° quarter-chord sweep and winglets. The selected section is NACA 2408, with a relative thickness of approximately 8.5%, estimated using the Torenbeek method after comparing with the Roskam chart. An equivalent trapezoidal taper ratio of 0.24 is used as an intermediate geometric representation. The final report gives root, kink and tip chords of 12.15 m, 8.878 m and 2.47 m respectively, with the kink located 4.040 m from the centreline. The mean aerodynamic chord is reported as 7.36 m.

High-lift and critical-case parameters. The final values include a maximum lift coefficient of 1.804 for take-off, 2.654 for landing, and a second-segment climb parameter of 0.144. The figures document the calculation path from individual performance curves to the combined design-point selection and then to the wing geometry.

05 — TEAM & CONTRIBUTION

Collaborative engineering work

The three reports were completed by a four-person team: Carolina Fernández Fernández, Lucía García Varela, Javier Bolaños Llano and Javier Lendínez Castillo. The reports document shared calculations and deliverables rather than assigning every task to an individual. I therefore present the technical results as team outcomes and describe my involvement as a contribution to the collective aircraft-design work, rather than claiming sole authorship of particular calculations.

06 — ENGINEERING TAKEAWAY

From requirements to a defensible preliminary configuration

These assignments show three complementary parts of aircraft design. The fuselage study translates a passenger mission into a practical cabin and external geometry. The cruise-polar study turns geometry and flow conditions into an aerodynamic model. The A350 redesign uses operating constraints to balance wing loading, installed thrust and planform geometry. The common engineering thread is traceability: every selected dimension or performance parameter should be connected to a requirement, a calculation method and a clearly stated assumption.
