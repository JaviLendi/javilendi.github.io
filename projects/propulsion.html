01 — THE CHALLENGE

Close an engine cycle before quoting performance

Thrust, specific fuel consumption, and thermal efficiency only make sense once every station obeys the same atmosphere, fuel, and component assumptions. In Resolución de un turborreactor con postcombustor I built that station model myself in Python and swept a restricted flight envelope, 0.25 < Mach < 0.95, looking for the operating region that maximised thrust.

The engine in that study is a two-spool turbojet: diffuser, low- and high-pressure compressors, combustor, two turbines, afterburner, and nozzles. I tracked stagnation pressure and temperature, mass flow, and stagnation enthalpy through the machine, then computed thrust, specific impulse, specific fuel consumption, and thermal, propulsive, and overall efficiency.

02 — CYCLE MODEL

Same gas model at every station

01 / ATMOSPHEREISA static pressure and temperature at the flight condition
02 / GASNASA polynomial cp, then γ from Mayer’s relation
03 / COMPONENTSAdiabatic efficiencies, pressure ratios, and turbine work balance
04 / PERFORMANCEThrust, impulse, SFC, and efficiency across the envelope

I solved stagnation relations in the diffuser, compressor pressure ratio and adiabatic efficiency, combustor pressure loss and efficiency, the turbine temperature drop required to drive each compressor, afterburner heat addition, and a nozzle with its own adiabatic efficiency. Mass flow at the nozzle closed the thrust calculation, including the pressure term when the exit was not perfectly expanded.

The Python script then mapped thermal, propulsive, and overall efficiency against altitude and speed, compressor delivery temperature, specific fuel consumption against thrust, and the high-pressure compressor map. I also wrote a short improvement note on afterburner use, diffuser area, nozzle temperature limit, and control logic. Those notes are design comments on the coursework engine, not a proposed modification to a flying powerplant.

I describe the model structure and the quantities it produced. I have not republished a single thrust or SFC figure here, because those values belong to one assumed component map and one design point inside the report.
03 — WORKSHOP ENGINES

Compare the ideal station model with a running engine

With Javier Bolaños Llano I tested an instrumented AMT Olympus HP gas turbine in the propulsion workshop. It is a single-stage centrifugal compressor, an annular combustor burning Jet-A1/JP-8, a single-stage axial turbine, and a convergent nozzle. We recorded stagnation pressure and temperature at the stations, fuel flow, thrust, spool speed, and ambient conditions, then corrected the measurements to a standard day.

The nominal study point in the report is 108,000 rpm. We solved the ideal cycle at that speed, repeated the calculation for the other steady speeds we ran, and compared calculated thrust, specific impulse, specific fuel consumption, air mass flow, and jet velocity with the measured traces. Compressor, turbine, and nozzle efficiencies were plotted against speed so the gap between the ideal station model and the rig was visible.

In a second workshop report, again with Javier Bolaños Llano, we identified the Honeywell TPE331-10 as installed in the propulsion laboratory and related it to a CASA C-212-class turboprop. The write-up follows the real machine: inlet and diffuser, two centrifugal compressor stages, reverse-flow annular combustor, three-stage turbine, gearbox, and exhaust. For a turboprop, the useful argument is that nozzle kinetic energy should stay small, because the turbine should absorb the gas energy and deliver it to the propeller.

Component assumptions used in our TPE331-10 coursework model. They are modelling inputs from the report, not a new engine test.

We estimated equivalent shaft power and fuel flow from the published TPE331 rating chart, then solved one nominal operating point. I do not repeat the report’s algebraic line that writes ESFC as the product of specific consumption and power; the ratings chart already separates equivalent specific fuel consumption from equivalent shaft horsepower, and that is the distinction I use.

04 — OTHER CYCLES

Otto sizing and a closed Stirling cycle

With Carolina Fernández Fernández, Lucía García Varela, and Javier Bolaños Llano I sized an automotive Otto engine in MATLAB. We fixed the design around 77 kW and a displacement of 1,580 cm³, using a Kia Niro 1.6 hybrid as the reference class, with a connecting-rod ratio of 0.3, stoichiometric-rich equivalence ratio 1.2, and a Wiebe combustion model. The sweeps covered cylinder count, bore and stroke, inlet-valve closing delay, exhaust opening advance, geometric compression ratio, and spark advance. The plots of power and brake mean effective pressure were the selection tool; the final table in the report is a coursework optimum for those constraints, not a built engine.

Earlier, with Ignacio Javier Hernández García and Ivan Pacheco Mínguez, I studied a Stirling engine: ideal isothermal and isochoric branches, regenerator, a real-gas correction, piston geometry, and a Python p–V diagram. The project closed with a solar-collector concept and a Beale-number estimate of power. It sits beside the gas-turbine work as an external-combustion cycle, where the heat addition is not inside the working gas.
